The first time someone called me *ambiciosa*, I was nineteen and I flinched.

It was a tía at a family lunch, and she did not mean it the way the LinkedIn posts mean it. She meant it the old way — the way that rhymes with *interesada*, with wanting more than your share, with a woman who has gotten above herself. I spent the rest of that lunch making myself smaller, laughing a little less loudly, agreeing a little faster.

It took me a decade to understand what happened at that table.

## The same word, two countries

There is a strange double life to ambition when you live between two languages.

In English, on a résumé, "ambitious" is something you put in a cover letter. In Spanish, at a Dominican kitchen table, it can still land like an accusation. We have inherited a grammar where a woman who wants things — money, a title, space, time alone — owes everyone an explanation.

> We were taught to want quietly, to achieve almost apologetically, to succeed without ever looking like we were trying.

I know women who run companies and still introduce themselves by their husband's last name first. I know women who closed six-figure deals and went home to apologize for working late. Not because anyone forbade them. Because the guilt was installed early, and it runs in the background like an app you can't quite force-close.

## Machismo doesn't always shout

We picture machismo as something loud — the catcall, the man who won't let his wife work. But the version that shaped most of us was quieter than that.

It was the brother who was never asked to wash a single plate. It was "she's so smart, *pero* she has a strong character." It was the way *seria* and *amargada* were used for the exact same woman depending on who was telling the story.

You can dismantle the loud machismo with a law. The quiet one you have to find inside your own decisions, one at a time.

## What I'm unlearning, out loud

I don't have this solved. But here is what I've started saying to myself, and to the younger women who ask:

- Wanting more is not the same as being ungrateful for what you have.
- You do not owe anyone an apology for taking up the room your work earned.
- "Difficult" is often just a woman with a boundary, described by someone who hit it.

The tía at that lunch is not a villain. She wanted too, once. Nobody gave her permission either, so she had none to hand down.

That's the part we get to change. We can be the generation that says *ambiciosa* the way it should have always been said — like a compliment, like a fact, like the most ordinary thing in the world.
